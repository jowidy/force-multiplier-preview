## Separate the software issue from the process issue

Customer relationship management software helps a team keep track of contacts, opportunities and follow-up. It becomes harder to use when fields do not match the work, stages mean different things to different people or responsibility is unclear.

Those problems can follow a business into a replacement system. Before choosing a new tool, describe what the current one fails to help the team do.

Be specific. "We cannot see who should follow up" points toward ownership and next-action rules. "Our records cannot accept the information this workflow requires" points toward a different kind of constraint.

## Trace the workflow before editing the settings

Choose a recent lead and map its route from entry to its current status. Identify the first person who saw it, the information they received and the decision that moved it forward.

Ask what makes the inquiry qualified, what happens when someone does not respond and when the record should change stage. Include the steps people handle outside the CRM, such as a phone call or a message in a separate inbox.

This map shows what the tool needs to support. It also exposes handoffs that changing a dashboard would leave untouched.

## Give every active record an owner and next step

For an active opportunity, the team should be able to see:

- Who is responsible for it.
- What needs to happen next.
- When that action is due.
- What would move it forward, put it on hold or close it.

If those answers live only in a meeting or someone's memory, start there. A record of past activity is useful, but it should also help the team decide what to do now.

## Keep fields that support a decision

A useful field serves a purpose: routing work, supporting a handoff, informing a report or supplying an automation with necessary context.

Review required fields with the people who complete them. If nobody uses a field, reconsider whether it belongs in the workflow. If a missing field repeatedly prevents a useful response, consider adding it at the point where the information becomes available.

Keep stage definitions equally concrete. Agree on what must have happened before a record moves to the next stage, and what evidence should be recorded.

## Define automation rules before enabling them

For each automated action, identify the trigger, owner, message, exception and stop condition. Establish how someone can see whether the action happened and correct it if necessary.

A reminder should reach the right person at a useful time. A follow-up message should stop when its reason no longer applies. Unclear rules are a process issue even when the software executes them correctly.

## Decide what actually needs to change

After the review, separate the improvements into changes to working practices, changes to configuration and genuine tool limitations. That gives a software decision a firmer basis.
