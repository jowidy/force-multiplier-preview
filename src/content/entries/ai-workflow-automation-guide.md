## Look for a recurring piece of work

Useful candidates often appear in ordinary administrative work: summarizing meeting notes, preparing a first response, categorizing inquiries or assembling a status report. These tasks already consume attention, so there is a real process to examine and compare.

Start by recording what repeats, who performs it and what makes a result useful. Also ask whether a simple rule or integration can handle the task without AI. A fixed reminder and a summary of an open-ended conversation need different kinds of support.

## Map the handoffs before choosing tools

Write down what starts the workflow, what information is available and who owns the next action. Identify the system that must be updated and how the result reaches the person who needs it.

For an inquiry summary, for example, the input could be a submitted form, the output a concise note, and the recipient the person responsible for replying. The workflow still needs a rule for incomplete information and a way to check the summary against the original.

This description gives the automation a job. It also helps you assess whether the tools you already use can support it.

## Keep review where judgment matters

AI can prepare information and drafts while a person remains responsible for important decisions. Useful arrangements include:

- A response is drafted for a person to review and send.
- Meeting notes are summarized, with action items confirmed by the meeting owner.
- Routine inquiries are categorized, while uncertain cases go to review.
- A report summary is prepared alongside the source figures for checking.

Make the review step visible. The person reviewing should know what was generated, which source it used and what they need to confirm.

## Design the exception path

The straightforward case is only part of the work. Test what happens when information is missing, a contact already exists or a request does not fit the expected categories.

Consider conflicting records, an unavailable owner and output that does not meet the agreed standard. Decide when the automation should stop, where the issue should appear and who will resolve it.

A useful exception message gives that person enough context to act. Quietly skipping a step can leave the business with less visibility than the manual process provided.

## Compare time and quality

Measure the whole task, including review and corrections. A quickly generated draft is not a time saving if it requires extensive repair.

Depending on the workflow, useful measures include handling time, completeness of records, missed handoffs, response time and the proportion of work with a visible next step. Keep examples of incorrect or incomplete output so the review includes weaknesses as well as successes.

Use the comparison to decide whether the process is worth continuing, needs revision or should stay manual.

## Choose a modest first project

Good candidates from the existing workflow might include an inquiry summary, action-item extraction from meeting notes, a follow-up reminder or a reporting summary with source links.

Choose one with a clear owner, accessible inputs and a result that is straightforward to inspect. Agree on the review habit before using it for continuing work.
